Investor Orientation and Commitment in Outcomes‐Based Public Service Contracts

ABSTRACT Outcomes‐based contracts (OBCs) organize public service delivery around measurable results, yet little is known about how different investors engage with these instruments. This article examines how investor orientation (social, financial, or dual‐purpose) shapes investor commitment in OBCs worldwide. Drawing on a global dataset of 338 contracts, we combine descriptive analyses, regression models, and qualitative interviews to investigate how investor orientation relates to investment scale. We hypothesize that OBCs involving socially oriented investors are associated with higher levels of investment than those involving financially or dual‐purpose investors. Consistent with this prediction, socially oriented investors consistently commit larger amounts, whereas financially oriented and dual‐purpose investors typically participate with smaller contributions. The “Professional Mortality” Problem in Early City Management: A Study of Organized Denial

ABSTRACT The early city management profession experienced appalling rates of attrition. This problem of “professional mortality” had been clearly recognized by the end of World War I: causes of attrition included threats and violence against managers, intense political interference in managers' work, and interpersonal missteps. Yet the organized management profession and advocates of the manager plan actively ignored threats and were unwilling to respond collectively or officially to political interference; leading scholars recognized but did not publicly discuss common interpersonal problems faced by managers. This silence on the underlying causes of turnover marked an effort to protect the manager plan's viability in the face of growing electoral opposition during the 1920s, placing the interests of the profession above its practicing members.
