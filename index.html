Skills, Not Just Will: Experimental Evidence From Uganda on Transparency in Public Procurement

ABSTRACT Governments are usually the largest purchasers of goods and services, creating significant opportunities for corruption. We study whether offering training and technical assistance to procurement officials in 50 Ugandan districts prompted them to publish procurement actions on an online public portal, as legally required. Among districts that received the training and technical assistance program, we randomized whether procurement officials received information in advance about their eligibility to earn a public award for adherence to these transparency standards. We use national administrative data across multiple financial years and 134 district governments to evaluate impacts. The training and technical assistance program increased the number and value of procurement actions uploaded several‐fold, while award eligibility did not have similarly large effects. Artificial Intelligence, Bureaucratic Discretion, and Democratic Administration

ABSTRACT Since the start of the COVID‐19 pandemic, many countries have been using artificial intelligence (AI) to replace civil service personnel. We argue that this practice undermines core democratic values. Using the public values framework, we identify four clusters of values that are threatened when bureaucratic discretion is transferred to algorithms: accountability, fairness and equity, individual consideration, and reason‐giving. Each of these values is incompatible with how algorithms render decisions. Because algorithms produce outputs based on statistical regularities, they diffuse responsibility across multiple parties, fail to make accurate predictions about anomalous cases, and attenuate citizens' claims to individualized consideration.
