Promises, Threats, and Norms: A Mixed Methods Study on Bureaucratic Support for Public Participation

ABSTRACT Public participation can enhance policy quality and democratic legitimacy but may threaten bureaucratic control. We hypothesize that bureaucratic support for participation is higher when policies are less complex and participants are broadly representative. Using a mixed‐methods design involving Indonesian local public servants, we pursue complementarity by combining a 2 × 2 vignette‐based survey experiment with a Gioia analysis of qualitative reflections to explain the experimental results and further illuminate how bureaucrats evaluate participation. The experiment shows that participant representativeness significantly increases support, whereas policy complexity has no statistically significant effect. Integrating the experimental and qualitative data through joint displays reveals that bureaucrats rejecting participation frequently cite participant‐related risks, whereas supporters emphasize instrumental, procedural, and implementation benefits. Governing Reserves: Managerial Discretion and Government Creditworthiness

ABSTRACT A government's ability to generate surpluses and build reserves reflects prudent financial management, with the accumulation of reserves signaling sound budgeting practices. Reserves are essential for sound fiscal governance during economic downturns, and managerial discretion over these resources is crucial to minimizing disruptions in service delivery. While the counter‐cyclical benefits of formal reserves are well documented, less attention has been paid to the discretion managers retain over reserves. Using credit ratings as a proxy for creditworthiness, we find that managerial discretion over accumulated resources enhances fiscal flexibility and strengthens creditworthiness; however, the presence of formal reserves alone does not improve credit ratings.
